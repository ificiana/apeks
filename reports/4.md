# Report 4: cargo, purposeful ships and port regrouping, 4 arms × 11 seeds

Runs from 2026-11-11: `uv run apeks-run experiments/*_sweep.toml --snapshot-every 10 --logs logs/r4`. Each arm ran seeds 0–10 for 6000 ticks, with the same scripted events as before: a storm at t=1500–2500 and the strait blocked at t=3000–4000. LLM arms use `claude-haiku-5-5`. All 44 runs finished, with 0 LLM failures and 0 skipped calls.

What differs from report 3 is that ships carry packages (`docs/plans/cargo.md`):

- Each port generates packages by a Poisson process at `DEMAND_RATE` = 0.01 per tick, with destinations uniform over the other ports.
- Ships hold 4 packages each. A fixed heuristic, the same in every arm, decides what each ship loads and where it sails next.
- Ships regroup into one-leg convoys only at ports, every 10 ticks. A convoy holds at most 8 ships and leaves when all are full or one has waited 100 ticks.
- Berths count ships. Unloading and loading take 5 ticks per package.
- All 16 ships start empty and docked.
- "Deliveries" now counts packages delivered, not berth arrivals.

The rest of the information model is the same as in report 3. Port stations also broadcast their backlog, and HQ's prompt shows the backlogs it has received. Backlogs never trigger an LLM call.

Demand depends only on the seed, so every arm faces the same packages: 304 per run on average (334 for seed 0 in all four arms). `DEMAND_RATE` was tuned with stub classical runs. At 0.01, classical's load factor is 0.73, within the 0.70–0.80 target. Classical deliveries level off near 180 at twice that demand, so the default sits close to classical's capacity.

## Inference

The method is the same as in report 3:

- Arms are paired by seed.
- The main test is the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with a paired t-test as a cross-check.
- Confidence intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals (10 000 resamples).
- The effect size is d_z.
- A Holm correction runs across the 30 comparisons against classical; ✓ means significant at 0.05 after it.

## Findings

1. **LLM routing's advantage grows with cargo.**
   - It delivers 90% more packages than classical, in all 11 seeds, and cuts package latency by 57%.
   - It keeps the mean backlog at about 8 packages, against classical's 53. That is enough to keep up with demand: about 10 packages are left at the end, against 108 for classical.
   - Energy per delivery halves, at the same total energy.
   - Congestion falls by three quarters, the first significant congestion effect in any report.

2. **Classical's routing turns cargo into queues.**
   - Classical ships make 61 berth arrivals per run, against 157 with LLM routing. Its legs are slow and its ships spend much of their time congested.
   - Waiting packages pile up, so departing ships are usually full: classical's load factor of 0.73 is the highest of any arm.
   - That high load factor is a symptom of slowness, not an efficiency.

3. **The LLM-routing arms sail emptier because they clear the ports.**
   - They have a lower load factor (0.55) and five times the empty sailing distance.
   - With little waiting, ships often leave after `MAX_WAIT` partly full, or sail empty towards a reported backlog.
   - The fixed heuristic, not routing, makes those choices. They are a consequence of the speed, not a separate cost: energy per delivery is still half of classical's.

4. **LLM formation is neutral for cargo, and still unsafe.**
   - Its deliveries, latency, load and energy match classical.
   - Its near misses rise by 65% (+92.5, worse in 9/11 seeds), which is not significant after Holm (p=0.010 against a threshold of 0.0019).
   - Formation is the source of the combined arm's extra near misses over LLM routing alone (+54, p=0.002). This is the same pattern as report 3.

5. **Beliefs are wrong for longer in the fast arms.** The LLM-routing arms log more false and missed ship-ticks than classical. The likely reason is that fast ships spend more time at sea, away from port notices. This was not checked against the traces.

## Limits

- 11 seeds, one map, one model, and one demand level close to classical's capacity. The size of the gap depends on that level, because classical saturates and LLM routing does not.
- The cargo heuristic and regrouping constants (`MAX_WAIT`, `MAX_CONVOY`, `REGROUP_EVERY`) were not varied. The load factor and empty distance depend on them directly.
- Radio and report noise share one stream per seed, but arms draw from it differently once their behaviour diverges. Only the map, events, storms, news and demand are identical across arms.
- An LLM run is not reproducible from its seed.

## Next steps

- Sweep `demand` (for example 0.5, 1, 2) to locate each arm's saturation point.
- Vary `MAX_WAIT` to trade load factor against latency, and check whether the LLM-routing advantage holds at higher loads.
- Trace the LLM-formation near misses. Check whether they happen between convoys on different policy versions or at port regrouping, where ships of several convoys hold close together.
